## Question

How can learning resolve uncertainty while enlarging the set of questions a learner can recognize and pursue? What observations would distinguish this expansion from increased epistemic motivation or reduced investigation costs?

## Claim to examine

An account of sustained curiosity needs to distinguish the value of inquiry, its costs, and the availability of intelligible questions. A sequence of answers can expand inquiry at unchanged motivation when each answer makes further questions accessible. Increased exploration alone cannot identify which mechanism operated. The proposed contribution is a causal and measurement distinction within existing learning-progress and knowledge-network accounts, not the discovery that answers sometimes provoke curiosity.

## Kind and limits

Conceptual synthesis with an explicit analytical account and a prospective experimental design. No original participants, biological experiments or empirical results are claimed. No simulation is required to establish a conditional branching calculation or an observational-equivalence argument; add one only if a substantive question needs it. Keep `has_simulation: false` unless that decision changes.

The title is **The Expanding Unknown: A Generative Account of Curiosity**. The argument concerns the organization and continuation of inquiry, not the evolutionary origin of motivation or an explanation of conscious experience. Educational authority and basal cognition are boundary cases. Do not convert a toy-learning result into a verdict on all schools, or information use into evidence that an organ feels curiosity.

## Closest alternatives and necessary literature

The strongest rival is that question availability is already represented by knowledge-network reconfiguration, learning progress, or expected future usefulness; a new motivational drive would add no explanatory value. Engage this possibility directly.

Source leads to inspect: Loewenstein's information-gap account; Schmidhuber's compression-progress theory; Oudeyer, Kaplan and Hafner on developmental intrinsic motivation; Dubey and Griffiths on future usefulness; Patankar and colleagues on filling, compressing and reconfiguring knowledge networks; Ten and colleagues on human learning progress; Liquin on effort versus reported curiosity; Abir and colleagues on reinforcement for related information; Bonawitz and colleagues on pedagogical inference; Mattingly and colleagues on bacterial information use; Levin and the Ertle–Levin–Scheutz maze study. Measurement sources must support a distinction between felt curiosity, information choices and learning outcomes.

## Planned argumentative movement

Begin with a concrete case in which an explanation makes a new question intelligible. Locate that change within the existing literature. Show why activity counts cannot distinguish motivation, costs and question availability, then derive the limits of the avalanche metaphor. Develop a design that measures question access before voluntary pursuit, uses a common question menu as a separate intervention, and treats unchanged ratings as inconclusive without an equivalence test. Return to education and biological interpretation only after specifying what the measures could establish.
